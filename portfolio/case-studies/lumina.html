Lumina — an AI learning assistant that adapts to how you're actually studying.

An end-to-end UI/UX design for a mobile AI tutor: onboarding and sign-in, a conversational chat tutor, and a level-based learning path that turns study progress into something visible and motivating.

01 — The problem

Learning apps tend to either overwhelm learners with content, or under-structure it.

Lumina is positioned as an "AI learning assistant" — a tutor a learner can talk to, rather than a static course library. The interface needed to do two things at once: feel approachable enough for a first-time user during onboarding, and organised enough to hold a full curriculum of topics, levels and progress once someone is a regular user.

The design needed to accommodate a learner who wants a quick answer to a specific question (via chat) as well as one who wants a structured path to follow over multiple sessions (via the level system) — without those two modes feeling like two different products.

02 — Design goals

What the interface needed to accomplish

  • Make first use effortless. A short, visual onboarding and a familiar sign-in pattern, so a new user reaches value in a few taps.
  • Give structure to open-ended learning. A level-based path (Basics → Intermediate → Advanced Project) so progress has a visible shape, not just a chat log.
  • Keep the AI tutor conversational, not clinical. A chat interface with contextual quick-replies, so asking a follow-up question feels as easy as texting.
  • Surface progress and next steps on return visits. A home dashboard that greets the learner by name and immediately shows what to do next.

05 — Design decisions

Why the interface looks and behaves the way it does

Typography

What: A confident, slightly rounded display face for headings ("Ignite Your Intelligence", level titles) paired with a compact sans for body copy and metadata.

Why: Learning content is dense — level names, descriptions, percentages, timestamps. A clear size and weight hierarchy lets a learner scan a screen (what level am I on, what's my progress) without reading every line.

Color

What: A single confident blue as the primary/brand color against a light, mostly white surface, with green used specifically for "unlocked / on-track" states and grey for locked content.

Why: Blue reads as trustworthy and calm rather than gamified — appropriate for a study tool used daily. Reserving color for status (locked vs. in-progress vs. complete) means color carries meaning instead of just decoration.

Layout

What: A vertical, card-based layout on the learning-path and home screens, with generous spacing between level cards and a fixed bottom tab bar for navigation.

Why: Each level card needs room for a title, short description, a progress bar or lock state, and a time estimate — stacking them vertically with clear separation keeps each level scannable on its own, which matters more here than fitting more on screen.

Components

What: Level-status cards with lock icons, a circular progress ring on the home dashboard, a goals checklist, and a chat bubble system with quick-reply chips.

Why: The lock icon does the job of explaining "you can't access this yet" without a paragraph of copy. The circular ring gives an instant sense of "mostly done" or "just started" at a glance, and quick-reply chips in chat lower the effort of asking a follow-up question.

Interaction design

What: A chat interface where the AI tutor initiates with a contextual message ("I see you were reviewing Quantum Mechanics earlier…") rather than a blank input field.

Why: An empty chat box is a blank-page problem — it puts the burden of starting on the learner. Having the assistant open with context lowers the barrier to continuing a session.

Potential improvement

Hover/press feedback states and a lesson-completion transition aren't visible in the static screens — worth defining explicitly in a future iteration so progression feels rewarded, not just recorded.

07 — Accessibility

Considerations built into the screens

Locked levels are communicated with both an icon and muted color, not color alone. Progress is shown numerically (percentages, "Day 4") alongside the visual ring or bar, so the information isn't lost for anyone who can't rely on the visual alone. Tap targets on level cards and tab bar icons are sized for comfortable mobile touch.

No formal accessibility audit or contrast testing was performed on this project — these are structural choices made during design, not a documented compliance pass.

08 — What I learned

Reflection

  • Designing a chat interface alongside a structured curriculum forced a real decision about hierarchy — Lumina treats chat as the "ask anything" layer and the level path as the "planned progress" layer, rather than trying to merge them into one screen.
  • Mapping the flow in Miro before touching Figma made the level-lock logic (what's visible vs. accessible) much clearer than it would have been designing screen-by-screen.
  • Next time, I'd define the transition/feedback state for completing a level explicitly, rather than leaving it implied by the static screens — progress systems work best when the moment of progress is felt, not just shown.
