## 4. What is distinctive

- The customer role (GTM engineer) is an ecosystem Clay created, and the people trained on Clay become both buyers and advocates.
- Usage-based pricing and a free entry point let users start without procurement.
- Community members' public work functions as marketing and demos.
- Agencies resell and implement, extending reach beyond Clay's own headcount.

## Lessons for a small B2B vendor selling a private on-prem LMS with in-network AI to universities and corporate L&D

1. Name and train a practitioner role. Clay built demand by certifying the people who use the product. Analog: certify "learning technologists" or admins on your LMS; they influence purchases and move between employers. Caveat: Clay's scale (35k Slack) is far beyond what a small vendor can match; start with a small cohort.
2. Make the proof public and low-friction, within your constraint. Clay's PLG relies on instant self-serve. On-prem cannot offer that, so substitute a hosted sandbox or recorded reverse demo using the buyer's own course content, and publish the results (with permission).
3. Use implementers as a channel. Agencies extend Clay. Analog: partner with instructional-design firms and campus IT integrators who deploy and support the install. Check the evidence: Clay's agency numbers are unverified, so test with two or three partners first.
4. Lead with the narrow wedge and the real differentiator. Clay focused on one use case. Your wedge is data staying inside the customer network (private AI). Target buyers where that is a hard requirement (research universities, regulated corporate training) rather than all L&D.
5. Do not copy what does not transfer. Clay's usage-based credits and free tier do not map to an on-prem license; its sales-cycle and pilot data were not found, so plan a paid pilot and a mutual action plan with security review as the key milestone rather than assuming Clay's patterns. Gather your own cycle-length data.
