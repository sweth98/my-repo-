## Lessons for a small B2B vendor selling a private on-prem LMS with in-network AI to universities and corporate L&D
1. Your privacy architecture is the product, not a checkbox. MagicSchool had to build a public trust hub and certifications because cloud AI worries buyers; in-network AI sidesteps the "who trains on our data" question. Lead with it, but back claims with documents (data-flow diagram, DPA, security questionnaire answers). Note MagicSchool's own conflicting SOC 2 wording shows the cost of vague claims: state exactly what you hold.
2. Bottom-up seeding needs a free surface, but on-prem limits it. You cannot offer a viral free signup; substitute a sandbox/hosted demo with synthetic data, or a free pilot course for one instructor or L&D team, to create a champion. Expect slower, lower-volume champion growth than MagicSchool's.
3. Pair the champion with a procurement-ready package. MagicSchool's revenue comes from district enterprise tiers (SSO, SIS/LMS integration, dashboards, DPA). Prepare SSO/SAML, SIS/HRIS integration, accessibility (VPAT) and a standard DPA before the first pilot so champions can forward it to IT and legal.
4. Run time-boxed, scoped pilots with success criteria. The one district-pilot-to-expansion signal (Hillsborough) is UNVERIFIED, but pilots are standard; define the metric (instructor hours saved, completion rate), the end date and the conversion price up front.
5. Borrow community tactics selectively. Educator-founder credibility, a power-user group with early access, and conference presence worked for MagicSchool; for you, pick one or two events (e.g. higher-ed and L&D shows) and a small advisory group of customers. Do not copy their scale claims: their numbers are self-reported, and MagicSchool's growth is a consumer-style outlier that a niche on-prem vendor will not replicate.
